# Thinking Point: Morse Code and the Origins of Serial Communication

Morse code offers an early example of modern serial communication. International Morse Code was standardised in **1865**, building on **Samuel Morse’s** work and later refinements. Like any protocol, it is an agreed set of rules for encoding and interpreting signals.

## From Morse to serial communication

Morse uses dots (`.`), dashes (`-`) and timed pauses to encode symbols, letters and words. Information travels sequentially over one channel—a wire or wireless link—and the receiver uses the same rules to reconstruct the message. Morse is an analogy, not a literal binary system: modern digital systems use **0s and 1s** (bits), while both rely on timing and shared interpretation.

| Morse communication | Embedded serial communication |
|---|---|
| Dots, dashes and pauses | Logic states, bits and timing/framing |
| Telegraph or radio channel | Electrical channel |
| Morse rules; operator decodes | Serial protocol; hardware/software decodes |

Morse became vital to maritime communication, from distress calls to routine messages. It was phased out of international maritime use in **1999**. The next two course modules explore serial protocols and their use in embedded systems.

## SOS at sea: the 1909 *Slavonia* rescue

The Morse distress signal is `... --- ...` (three short, three long, three short). The course’s cargo-ship emergency is a thought experiment; the historical investigation points to the **RMS Slavonia**. On **10 June 1909**, while sailing from New York to Trieste, it ran aground off **Flores in the Azores** and sent SOS—the first recorded use of the signal in a shipwreck. The *Prinzess Irene* responded that day and rescued 110 passengers; the *Batavia* rescued the remaining 300 the next day. All passengers were saved, though the ship was lost.

The rescuers were the heroes: their ships’ crews and captains answered the call. There were no villains in the sense of deliberate wrongdoing, but the inquiry blamed *Slavonia*’s captain, **A. G. Dunning**, for an error of judgment that led to the grounding.

A separate 1909 milestone was the **SS Arapahoe**, disabled near **Cape Hatteras, North Carolina**, on 11 August. Wireless operator **Theodore Haubner** sent SOS in what is recognised as the first American ship-to-shore SOS. This is distinct from the earlier *Slavonia* shipwreck call.

> **Key takeaway:** Serial communication sends agreed signals in sequence over a channel and lets a receiver reconstruct the message. Embedded systems apply the same principle using digital bits, precise timing and electronic hardware.
